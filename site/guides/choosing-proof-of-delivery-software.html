How to Choose Proof of Delivery Software: A Buyer’s Checklist

Every vendor says their proof of delivery software is “easy,” “complete,” and “built for teams like yours.” Those words are free. This checklist is what to look for underneath them — the criteria that separate software your drivers will actually use from software you pay for while everyone quietly goes back to paper.

Start with your operation, not the software

Before comparing vendors, answer one question honestly: what problem are you buying your way out of? The right software for a team drowning in “where is my order?” calls is not the same as the right software for a team losing chargeback disputes. Common buying motives:

  • Disputed deliveries you can’t win without evidence.
  • Status calls pulling dispatch off real work.
  • No visibility into where drivers are during the day.
  • Paper proof that’s lost, illegible, or unfindable when it matters.
  • Growth — the phone-call system worked at three drivers and is breaking at ten.

Write your motive down. Every criterion below should be scored against it — a feature that doesn’t serve your motive is a feature you’ll pay for and never use.

The checklist

1. Capture quality

Ask exactly what a completed delivery record contains: photos (how many, what resolution, are they compressed beyond usefulness?), e-signatures (legible, timestamped, tied to the stop?), and driver notes. Then ask the harder question: what does the record look like six months later when you need it for a dispute? Thumbnails that looked fine on a phone screen can be useless in a chargeback fight. If the vendor won’t show you a full record, that’s your answer.

2. GPS and timestamp integrity

The timestamp and location should be captured automatically by the device, not entered or editable by the driver. Anything a rushed driver has to type will eventually be skipped or wrong, and anything editable after the fact is questionable evidence. Good software also flags the records that don’t add up — a “delivery” logged blocks from the address — rather than quietly accepting them.

3. Searchability

This is where most software dies in practice. Ask: can I find a specific delivery by address, date, driver, or order number in under thirty seconds? Then try it with the vendor’s demo data. A record you can’t retrieve when a customer is disputing in real time is barely a record at all. Export matters here too — can you get your data out as CSV or PDF when an accountant or a lawyer asks?

4. Driver learning curve

Your drivers will make or break this purchase. The app should be learnable in minutes: a clear stop list, big tap targets, one obvious flow per stop. Ask how long onboarding takes for a non-technical driver, and whether it works on the drivers’ own phones — requiring company-issued devices adds a hardware budget most small operations didn’t plan for. If the demo takes fifteen minutes to explain, your drivers will hate it on day one.

5. Customer experience

The customer-facing side matters as much as the driver side. Tracking links must open in a normal browser with no app install — every extra step costs you the customers who need reassurance most. Notifications should go out by text or email automatically: dispatched, en route, delivered with proof. Ask the vendor to walk you through exactly what the customer sees, because that’s what your customers will judge you by.

6. Data ownership and export

Whose records are they — yours or the vendor’s? Ask: if you cancel, do your delivery records come with you, and in what format? Is there an API or at least bulk CSV export? What happens to your history on day one of cancellation? Vendors who are vague here are telling you something important. Your delivery history is operational evidence and, in disputes, a legal asset. Don’t rent it without an exit plan.

7. Pricing model

Understand exactly what you pay for: per driver, per delivery, per month. Then do the math for your fleet — $15/driver/month means something very different at 3 drivers than at 30. Ask what the free tier actually includes, what breaks when you outgrow it, and whether the price scales with features you don’t need. Free isn’t automatically better — but a free tier you can genuinely run on beats a cheap plan with surprise overage fees.

8. Offline behavior

Drivers hit dead zones — rural routes, parking garages, warehouse basements. Ask what the app does with no signal: does it queue records locally and sync later, or does it refuse to work? Can the driver still complete stops, capture photos, and get signatures offline? If the vendor hedges on this question, test it yourself in the trial. Dead-zone behavior is invisible in a sales demo and very visible on a Tuesday in February.

The two-minute trial: the single most informative thing you can do is put the app in a driver’s hands for two days on a real route. Everything in the checklist above is easier to feel than to read — and a vendor that won’t let you try it before you pay has already told you their answer.

Red flags

Walk away — or at least slow down — when you see these:

  • No real trial. A demo you can’t put on your drivers’ phones isn’t a trial, it’s a presentation.
  • No export path. Your data, their server, no way out — you’re renting, not owning.
  • Proprietary hardware required. For most delivery operations, the driver’s phone is the right device. Required hardware is a cost and a failure point.
  • Guarantees of AI magic. Software that “predicts” or “eliminates” delivery problems is selling a story. Proof of delivery is an evidence business; promises beyond evidence deserve skepticism.
  • Feature lists instead of workflows. Vendors show checkboxes; your business runs workflows. If they can’t show you a driver’s actual stop-by-stop flow, they’re selling software, not a solution.

Questions to ask every vendor

  1. Show me a complete delivery record — what the customer disputes with, not the dashboard.
  2. How does this work with no signal for an hour?
  3. What does a non-technical driver learn in the first ten minutes?
  4. If I cancel in a year, what happens to my records, exactly?
  5. What does the customer see — walk me through the tracking link and the delivery notification.
  6. What does this cost at my driver count, with every fee and limit included?
