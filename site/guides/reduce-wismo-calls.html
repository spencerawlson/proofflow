How to Reduce “Where Is My Order?” Calls

Every “where is my order?” call is a symptom, not an event. It means the customer ran out of information before the delivery ran its course — and the fix is almost never a better phone manner. It’s giving them the answer before they pick up the phone.

Why WISMO calls happen

WISMO calls — “where is my order” calls — come from exactly one place: the silence between dispatch and delivery. From the moment a customer places an order to the moment it arrives, they have no visibility into what’s happening. The common triggers:

  • The delivery window is vague. “Sometime Tuesday” is not information. A customer who has rearranged their day around a delivery and heard nothing by noon will call.
  • The window passed. A delivery that’s an hour late with no update feels like a delivery that will never come. Customers call not because they’re impatient, but because silence looks like abandonment.
  • Something went wrong without a message. The driver couldn’t find the address, the recipient wasn’t home, the item didn’t fit — and nobody told the customer. From their side, the delivery simply vanished.
  • The first delivery already failed once. Customers who have been burned before call earlier and more often. One bad experience creates a chronic caller.

Notice what’s missing from this list: customers are rarely calling because they enjoy it. They call because you haven’t told them something they need to know.

What WISMO calls actually cost you

The damage isn’t the phone bill. It’s what the call does to your operation while it’s happening:

  • Dispatch stops dispatching. Every status call pulls whoever answers — often the dispatcher — off real work: building routes, handling exceptions, answering the important calls. In a small operation, one person juggling both jobs means every status call delays everything else.
  • Answers require investigations. “Where’s my order?” can’t be answered from memory. Somebody has to find the order, figure out which route it’s on, reach the driver, and call back. A two-minute call becomes a twenty-minute interruption across two people.
  • They repeat. The same customer calls again tomorrow, because nothing about the experience changed. Status calls are the only category of customer contact that grows by itself.

No published statistic will tell you what this costs your operation — the numbers floating around the industry average in giants with dedicated support teams, not five-driver fleets. The honest measurement: count them for a week. Tally every status call, note how long each one took to resolve, and you’ll have the only number that matters.

The fixes that actually work

Everything that reduces WISMO calls follows one principle: never let the customer run out of information. Here’s how that plays out in practice.

1. Send proactive delivery notifications

Don’t wait for the customer to ask — tell them first. A simple sequence works: a message when the delivery is dispatched with the delivery window, a message when the driver is en route, and a confirmation when it arrives. Three short texts eliminate the three moments when customers most often reach for the phone. The content matters less than the timing; customers forgive almost anything as long as they hear it before they ask.

2. Give them a live tracking link

A tracking link with a live map is the single strongest WISMO reducer most businesses can deploy. A customer who can see the driver’s dot moving toward their street does not call — watching is its own reassurance. The critical detail: it must require no app install. Every extra step between the text and the map costs you the customers who need it most.

3. Keep delivery windows tight and honest

A four-hour window is a promise you can’t keep; a two-hour window you can keep is a gift. More important than the size of the window is what happens when it slips: a customer told at 1 PM that their 2 PM delivery is now 3:30 PM almost never calls. The same customer told nothing at 4 PM always does. Early bad news beats late silence, every time.

4. Confirm with proof, not just a message

“Your delivery is complete” is a claim. A photo of the package at the door, a signature, a GPS timestamp — that’s proof, and it changes the customer’s behavior after delivery as well as before it. Proof confirmations do double duty: they stop the anxious “did it actually arrive?” call, and they end the “it never arrived” dispute before it starts. See our guide on what proof of delivery is for the full picture.

5. Document exceptions visibly

A failed delivery with a message — “we attempted delivery at 2:14 PM, no one answered, here’s a photo of the door” — keeps the customer informed and protects you. A failed delivery with no message creates the most damaging WISMO call of all: the customer who knows something went wrong but doesn’t know what. Exception records cost the driver seconds and save dispatch hours.

The rule of thumb: if the customer knows less about their delivery than your dispatcher does, you have a WISMO problem. Every fix in this guide is just a way of closing that gap.

A sample notification sequence

Put together, the fixes look like this for a single delivery:

  1. Morning: “Your delivery is on its way today between 1–3 PM. Track it live: [link]”
  2. En route: the tracking link shows the driver’s live position on the route.
  3. If delayed: “Running about 45 minutes behind — new estimate 3:30 PM. Sorry about the wait.”
  4. On arrival: “Delivered at 2:41 PM. Photo proof: [link]. Signed by: M. Tremblay.”

At no point does this customer need to call you. That’s the entire point.

The bottom line

WISMO calls aren’t a customer-service problem. They’re an information problem with a customer-service symptom — and the cure is giving customers the information before they ask. Count your status calls for a week, deploy the notification sequence, count again. The gap between those two numbers is the time you just bought back.
